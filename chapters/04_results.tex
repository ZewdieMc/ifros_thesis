\subsection{Hardware and Platform}
\label{sec:hw_platform}
\paragraph{\textnormal{The custom datasets were collected using the AgileX Hunter 2.0 mobile robot equipped with an Ouster OS1-128 LiDAR. The sensor provides 128 scan lines, a 360° horizontal field of view, and operates at 10 Hz. It also includes a built-in IMU, which was used for LIO odometry estimation. The extrinsic calibration between the ouster LiDAR and the IMU can be computed online with FAST-LIO2, however in our experiments we used identity for rotation with no translation. All data were recorded using ROS 2 bag files for both real-time and offline processing. For running the mapping system, both in real-time and during offline refinement, a separate computer was used. It featured an AMD Ryzen 7 CPU, NVIDIA RTX 3060 GPU, and 32 GB RAM, running Ubuntu 22.04 with ROS 2 Humble. All mapping modules were implemented in C++ to ensure real-time performance and efficient offline processing.
}}

\subsection{Dataset}
\label{sec:dataset}

To evaluate the performance of the proposed mapping system, experiments were performed with various open source dataset and custom dataset collected around Saxion buildings. The custom datasets containing four sequences were collected in different days, with varying trajectory length, and number of loop closures. Three of the sequences denoted as Saxion-00, Saxion-01, and Saxion-03 were recorded in outdoor environment while one sequence referred to as Saxion-11 was recorded in indoor. Saxion-01 is the shortest of the outdoor trajectories and contains places revisited only in the same direction. Saxion-02 includes both reverse direction and lane-level lateral shift in revisited areas. Saxion-03 contains the longest trajectory with the highest number of loop closures.\\

	 Public datasets, MulRan\footnote{https://sites.google.com/view/mulran-pr/dataset} \cite{gskim-2020-mulran} and KITTI\footnote{https://www.cvlibs.net/datasets/kitti/} \cite{Geiger2013IJRR}, were used to evaluate the robustness of the proposed mapping module  to different variety of sensors types, trajectory length, and driving speed. These open source dataset also contain ground truth trajectories that are used for comparing the performance of the proposed method. The sequences KAIST02, KAIST03, and RiverSide01 from MulRan and sequences 00, and 05 from KITTI are used in the experimentation. \section{Evaluation metrics}
\label{sec:ev_metrics}
\paragraph{\textnormal{The proposed mapping pipeline was evaluated using a comprehensive set of quantitative and qualitative metrics to ensure a robust performance assessment. Specifically, \gls{ape} was employed to evaluate the global consistency of the estimated trajectory by comparing it against the ground truth data. To measure local accuracy and drift between consecutive poses, Relative Pose Error (RPE) was employed. To compute the APE, and RPE values of two trajectories, the Evo \cite{grupp2017evo} tool that supports trajectories in different file formats was used. All the APE values in our experiments are computed after aligning the trajectories using Evo. }}

\paragraph{\textnormal{Map consistency was assessed qualitatively through visual inspection of the generated maps and quantitatively using \gls{mme} \cite{7324196}. It computes the natural logarithm of the determinant of the covariance matrix. The smaller the MME value is, the more consistent the map is. Finally, Runtime Performance was measured by recording the average processing time per LiDAR scan in the online module. These evaluation criteria collectively provide a thorough insight into the accuracy, robustness, and efficiency of the developed system.
}}

\subsection{Map quality and consistency}
\paragraph{\textnormal{The evaluation of map accuracy and global consistency was conducted by analyzing the outputs generated after each stage of the mapping pipeline for the custom and public datasets. In the initial stage, maps generated using FAST-LIO2 alone demonstrated locally smooth and accurate map, especially in short trajectories. For instance, for saxion01 dataset, the map in Figure~\textcolor{blue}{\ref{fig:saxion01_map}a} showed minimal distortion within the sequential sub-segments. This is due the tightly coupling of LiDAR and IMU. However cumulative pose error caused noticeable misalignment in the loop closure regions. Specifically, a lateral misalignment of up to 9m(Table~\ref{tab:traj_error01}) was observed, indicating the limitation of FAST-LIO2 mapping.}}

\paragraph{\textnormal{Upon incorporating PGO, the system demonstrated significant correction in the loop closure areas bringing the lateral misalignment down to 2.5m, see Figure~\textcolor{blue}{\ref{fig:saxion01_map}b}, for saxion01 dataset. The vertical misalignment in the map (Figure~\textcolor{blue}{\ref{fig:saxion01_map}c}) and in the trajectory (Figure \ref{fig:saxion01_traj_xyz}) was also significantly corrected. This vertical drift is common in most LIO methods and we incorporated GPS factor into the PGO only considering the altitude difference as a factor and putting large values for the x, and y in the covariance matrix . The map after correcting the vertical misalignment is shown in Figure~\textcolor{blue}{\ref{fig:saxion01_map}d}. The PGO map have small misalignment caused by inaccuracies in the loop closure and incorrect relative pose (constraints) between nodes in the pose graph.
}}

\paragraph{\textnormal{The application of HBA to the frames (poses) generated by PGO in the offline phase yielded notable improvement in the map quality (Figure~\textcolor{blue}{\ref{fig:saxion01_map}e}) and trajectory(Figure~\ref{fig:saxion01_traj}). The residual distortions that persisted after PGO are corrected and refined. In the saxion01 dataset, the 2.5m lateral misalignment was eliminated, see Figure~\textcolor{blue}{\ref{fig:saxion01_map}e} , after applying HBA. Figure \ref{fig:saxion03_gmap} shows the final mapping result for the \texttt{saxion03} dataset.
}}

\subsection{Runtime performance}
\paragraph{\textnormal{The three components of the entire mapping system are evaluated for runtime performance based on their time consumption. FAST-LIO2 runs at a frequency that is equivalent to the sensor frame rate and outputs odometery at 10HZ. The total processing time per scan includes the time taken by motion compensation, state estimation, KNN search, and mapping. To further reduce the time consumption, we disabled the mapping step and only used the it to output odometery. Total of 16 cores were used to search for the closest surface and residual computation in the IEKF step. This helped to speed up the process by utilizing multi-threading. The average per scan processing time for FAST-LIO2 was therefore 2.5$\mathit{ms}$.}}
